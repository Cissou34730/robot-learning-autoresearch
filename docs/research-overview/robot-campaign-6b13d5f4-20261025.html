1. What the robot had to do

A simulated two-joint arm had to reach targets 6 to 20 cm from its base, in any direction. Its hand then had to stay within 1 cm of the target for two uninterrupted seconds: 100 control steps. Each official episode allowed at most 500 steps.

The goal was at least 196 successes in the fixed official panel of 200 episodes. A panel is a set of test episodes. Development panels help select a candidate; they are not added to the official score.

The PI is the AI principal investigator. An analyst writes the initial physical model. Coder and reviewer agents help with implementation. Runner executes accepted operations and records results; the PI makes the scientific decisions.

2. Three small changes, rather than a long plateau

  1. T1: train across the whole target range. The PI widened training from 14-20 cm to the task's full 6-20 cm range. The learning algorithm, observations, reward and direct motor-action mapping stayed unchanged. T1 scored 192/200 on the protected task-reference panel. Earlier checkpoints were worse.
  2. Find out why the hold fails. M3 replayed those same 200 situations in the research evaluator. It reproduced the same eight failed episodes. Two never reached tolerance; six entered it but repeatedly broke the hold. The protected panel and research replay are two views of the same situations, not 400 independent tests.
  3. T2: remove credit for a broken hold. One reward term changed: when the arm left tolerance before completing a hold, it lost the credit earned by that incomplete hold. The PI continued training from T1. The first fresh comparison improved, but the next panel showed a much smaller advantage.
  4. Inspect the remaining failures before choosing another change. On M5, all 11 T2 failures lay between about -150 and -122 degrees. Nine never reached tolerance. The remaining weakness was strongly directional, not spread evenly around the robot.
  5. T3: use mirror symmetry. The PI changed the policy's coordinate mapping and continued training from T2. On a fresh matched panel, T3 scored 400/400 against T2's 392/400. It then passed the separate official assessment at 199/200.

What the mirror mapping does

For a target below the x-axis, show the network the corresponding mirrored arm state and target above it. Map the network's two motor commands back by reversing their signs. The observation's two inverse-kinematics branch-offset pairs are also exchanged and reflected.

The actual target and task do not change. The network sees a more consistent representation of physically equivalent situations.

The network still decides each command

The final action mapping only keeps or reverses the network outputs. It does not use an analytic planner or trajectory controller to produce the motor commands.

This differs from the previous campaign's failed actor-output projection, which combined commands from an existing policy. The new method uses one canonical input representation and then trains with that mapping.

Each run requested 120,000 steps and completed 120,832 because training ended at a rollout boundary. The campaign used 362,496 steps in total. The selected checkpoint is 35,840 steps into T3, not the total training used by the campaign.

The location of the gain matters. All eight T2 failures on M6 lay in the same broad negative-angle sector found on M5. In the fixed sector from -180 to -90 degrees, T2 scored 90/98 and T3 scored 98/98. Both succeeded on all 302 episodes outside it.

This matches the proposed explanation better than a gain scattered across unrelated situations. The sector was chosen after looking at M5, but M6 used fresh episodes.

Statistical detail, without changing the main conclusion

The exact two-sided paired sign-test p values are 0.000061 for M4's selected T2 versus T1, 0.508 for M5, and 0.0078 for M6. These tests compare which candidate wins when their outcomes differ. A small p value is evidence of a difference in that comparison; it is not the probability that a method is correct, and it does not identify the cause.

T3's checkpoint was selected from training records before M6, rather than by comparing many T3 checkpoints on that panel. Its 400/400 result gave a 95% Wilson interval of about 99.05-100% and a modeled 99.6% chance of reaching at least 196/200 on a new panel. That calculation assumed independent representative episodes and a uniform prior. The official assessment then supplied a separate result.

4. Was it just luck?

An easier final test panel? Not enough to explain it.

T2 and T3 faced exactly the same fresh M6 targets. T3 recovered eight failures without losing a T2 success. The separate official panel also showed strong performance. This is more convincing than a single unpaired high score.

A different starting seed? No.

Both this campaign and the previous one started from the same baseline recipe and trained T1 with seed 0. Their target-range definitions were equivalent. A newly chosen starting seed does not explain the turnaround.

This does not establish identical model weights. The saved candidate fingerprints differ, and the initial evaluation panels were different.

A favorable fine-tuning run? Still possible.

T3 changed the mirror mapping, received more training and used training seed 2. There was no comparison with the same amount of continued training under unchanged policy I/O. The method was not repeated across training seeds.

The result supports the mapping-plus-training package, not a separately measured effect of the mapping alone. The T2 reward change has the same training-versus-method limitation.

A better sequence of PI decisions? Quite plausible.

The initial physical model in this run explicitly named reflection symmetry. The previous model did not. The PI then used that fact with the measured failure pattern and chose a useful intervention early.

That is better scientific behavior. It does not establish how often the same harness and instructions will produce it.

Verdict: the evaluated candidate's improvement is credible. The much cheaper campaign is promising, but its repeatability is unknown. Neither the policy method nor the complete campaign was replicated under a fixed setup.

7. What to retain, and what remains open

Good behavior worth retaining

  • Inspect actual failure records before choosing the next intervention.
  • Weaken an interpretation when a fresh panel gives weaker evidence.
  • Use physical structure to make the learning problem more consistent.
  • Test candidate and reference on the same fresh situations.
  • Request the official assessment when the evidence is strong enough, rather than requiring complete understanding first.

Questions this run does not settle

  • How often does this training method succeed across seeds?
  • How much of T3's gain came from the mapping, rather than continued training?
  • How often will the same harness find a short and productive research path?
  • Will the PI recover the right scientific context after compaction?

The most useful missing evidence: repeated training of the same method, and a continued-training comparison with unchanged policy I/O. Another large panel for this one checkpoint would mainly refine its rate; it would not resolve training repeatability or explain the cause of the gain.

These are possible follow-up comparisons, not new campaign requirements. This report uses existing evidence only; no new training or evaluation was run.
